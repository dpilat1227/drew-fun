Act I: The Monolith Meltdown — Jeff Bezos and the Black Friday Nightmare

In the early 2000s, Amazon.com was nearly assassinated by its own success.

Every November, as holiday shoppers flooded the website, Jeff Bezos watched his database engineers sweat through their shirts. Amazon's entire global storefront ran on a towering, multi-million-dollar monolithic database system engineered by Sun Microsystems and Oracle. It had 64 enterprise CPU cores, half a terabyte of RAM, and sounded like a commercial jet engine.

For twelve intense hours on Black Friday, that colossus ran at 88% capacity. It was an engineering marvel.

Then came Tuesday morning. For the other 364 days of the year, that multi-million-dollar monument sat in the dark at 3% CPU utilization. It burned thousands of kilowatts of electricity, sucked up datacenter refrigeration, and depreciated. Worse: during one holiday rush, an unhandled database lock locked the entire system. Because it was a single centralized machine, every checkout cart on Earth froze simultaneously.

Amazon's newly hired Chief Technology Officer, Werner Vogels, diagnosed the disease: Amazon was trapped in Scale-Up (Vertical Scaling).

  1. The Physical Silicon Ceiling: You cannot make one motherboard infinitely large.
  2. The Cost Wall: A server twice as big costs ten times as much.
  3. The Single Point of Failure (SPOF): When the Goliath dies, the kingdom falls.
"Failures are a given: everything fails, all the time."
— Werner Vogels, CTO of Amazon

The only path forward was Scale-Out (Horizontal Scaling): abandoning the luxury giant and building an architecture where software assumed every single server was cheap, disposable, and destined to die.

Act II: The Great Netflix Migration — Reed Hastings Bets on the Swarm

In August 2008, Netflix was still primarily a red-envelope DVD rental company starting to experiment with streaming. Suddenly, a massive hardware failure struck their private datacenter: a centralized storage area network (SAN) suffered severe database corruption.

For three straight days, Netflix could not ship a single DVD to any customer in America.

CEO Reed Hastings gathered his executive team. Most companies would have doubled down on bigger, more expensive datacenter gear. Hastings did the opposite. He made an unprecedented bet: Netflix would shut down all its private datacenters and migrate 100% of its infrastructure to Amazon Web Services.

Silicon Valley thought Hastings was crazy. Why trust your core business to your fiercest retail competitor? The answer became a masterclass in distributed systems:

  • The Elastic Swarm: Instead of buying servers for peak evening binge-watching hours, Netflix spun up thousands of EC2 instances at 7:00 PM using an Elastic Load Balancer (ELB), and shut them down at 1:00 AM so they stopped paying Amazon.
  • Asynchronous Decoupling (SQS): Netflix decoupled its user interface from backend video encoding using Amazon Simple Queue Service (SQS). If transcoding workers choked, customer streams never buffered.
  • Chaos Monkey: Netflix engineered a software tool called Chaos Monkey that ran inside their production cloud, randomly shooting and terminating live EC2 servers during business hours. Because their system was built for horizontal scale-out, the swarm healed itself instantly. The user on their living room couch never noticed a single dropped frame.

The Hidden Trap: Distributed Systems Are Brutal

  • The Network is Not Reliable: Packets drop, switches overheat, and latency is never zero.
  • No Global Clock: In an AWS datacenter with 50,000 servers, no two machines agree on the exact microsecond. Scalable systems never assume a global synchronized clock.
  • Tanenbaum’s Decentralization Law: Highly scalable systems require no centralized algorithms, no single machine holding global state, and nodes making decisions using local information.
  • REST Won Over RPC: In the 1990s, engineers tried Remote Procedure Calls (RPC)—forcing a network call across the continent to pretend it was a local function user = get_user(). When fiber cables flapped, threads hung indefinitely. Modern cloud systems embrace RESTful HTTP web services: asynchronous, stateless, using explicit HTTP status codes (200 OK, 404 Not Found, 500 Server Error) that acknowledge that over the wire, failure is always an option.

Act III: The UChicago Connection — Ian Foster and the Invention of Utility Compute

Where did the idea of the "cloud" actually come from? The intellectual DNA of cloud computing was forged right here in Hyde Park at the University of Chicago and Argonne National Laboratory.

In 1998, UChicago computer science professor Ian Foster authored the seminal book that earned him the title "the father of the Grid." Foster looked at the electrical power grid: when you plug a lamp into a wall socket, you don't care which hydroelectric dam or coal plant generated the electron. You don't negotiate a contract with a technician; you just draw current and pay for the kilowatt-hours.

Foster asked: Why can't computing work the exact same way? He defined Grid Computing as:

"A hardware and software infrastructure that provides dependable, consistent, pervasive, and inexpensive access to high-end computational capabilities."
— Ian Foster, 1998

In 2006, Amazon took Ian Foster's grid vision, stripped away the academic complexity, combined it with lightweight REST Web Services, and created AWS. As Professor Vasiliadis summarizes in lecture:

Cloud Computing ≈ Web Services + Grid Computing

Act V: The Ghost Disk and the $50 Million Ephemeral Disaster

In 2012, an algorithmic trading startup deployed a high-frequency trading bot on an AWS EC2 instance. The developer wanted maximum speed, so they stored their market historical data and transaction cache on the local disk drive provided with the instance.

On Friday night, to save money over the weekend, the junior developer clicked "Stop Instance."

On Monday morning, the developer clicked "Start Instance." They logged in. The disk was completely blank. Fifty million dollars of proprietary cache history had been erased.

The developer had fallen into the most infamous trap in cloud computing: The difference between Instance Store and Elastic Block Store (EBS).

1. Ephemeral Storage (Instance Store)

An actual NVMe SSD bolted directly onto the motherboard of your host blade. Ultra-fast (<100µs latency). But ephemeral (temporary). The moment you click "Stop," or the host blade suffers a power failure, every byte is permanently destroyed. Use only for scratch buffers and cache layers.

2. Elastic Block Store (EBS)

A virtual hard drive living on a dedicated storage cluster across the datacenter room, connected via fiber-optic Ethernet.

  • Volume Mobility: Detach from VM-1 and attach to VM-2 in seconds.
  • Persistence & Durability: Survives VM reboots, crashes, and shutdowns. Replicated automatically inside its AZ.
  • Programmatic Elasticity: Grow from 8 GB to 100 GB via API while the filesystem is running live.
  • S3 Snapshots: Point-in-time backups pushed to S3. First snapshot is a full clone; subsequent ones are incremental.
The 3 EBS Gotchas That Trip Students Up on the Quiz:
  1. The Same-AZ Rule: EBS volume and EC2 instance MUST be in the exact same Availability Zone! (Cannot attach a us-east-1a EBS volume to a us-east-1b EC2 instance).
  2. Provisioned Capacity Billing: You pay for provisioned capacity, not used space. (Allocate 100 GB, use 1 GB = billed for 100 GB).
  3. Single Attachment: Standard EBS volumes attach to only one instance at a time.

Q1: Advantages of Virtualized Block Storage (EBS)

(Teased directly by Professor Vasiliadis in Ed Discussion!)

✅ A. Mobility of volumes between virtual machines.
✅ B. Programmatic expansion of volume capacity without taking the volume offline.
✅ C. Independent lifecycle allowing data persistence even when the attached VM is stopped or terminated.
❌ D. Zero network latency compared to locally attached NVMe instance store SSDs.

⚠️ Penalty Trap Alert (-0.5 pts): Do NOT select D. EBS is network-attached over fiber (1–4ms latency), whereas physical Instance Store is locally attached to the bus (<100µs).

(Teased directly by Professor Vasiliadis in Ed Discussion!)

✅ A. An online wedding gift registry with highly variable seasonal web traffic.
✅ B. An instant messaging application handling millions of concurrent user connections.
❌ C. A single-threaded legacy scientific computation that cannot be parallelized across nodes.

Q3: Distributed Systems Scalability Principles

✅ A. Elimination of centralized algorithms, data structures, and services.
❌ B. Ensuring all nodes synchronize their operations with a centralized master clock.
✅ C. Decisions made independently by individual nodes based on local state.
✅ D. Asynchronous communications to decouple components and hide network latency.
❌ E. Reliance on high-bandwidth scale-up hardware for all coordinator nodes.
⚠️ Penalty Trap Alert (-0.5 pts): Do NOT select B. Global clocks are impossible in large distributed networks. Scalable systems never assume a global clock.

Q4: Distributed System Transparencies

✅ A. Location transparency hides where a resource is physically located.
✅ B. Migration transparency hides that a resource may move to another location.
❌ C. Access transparency hides that multiple identical copies of a resource exist.
✅ D. Concurrency transparency hides that a resource is being shared simultaneously by competing users.
✅ E. Failure transparency hides the failure and recovery of resources to maintain quality of service.
⚠️ Penalty Trap Alert (-0.5 pts): Do NOT select C. Hiding identical copies is Replication transparency. Access transparency hides differences in machine representation and access protocols.

Q5: AWS Elastic Block Store (EBS) Architectural Constraints

✅ A. An EBS volume and the EC2 instance it attaches to must reside in the exact same Availability Zone (AZ).
❌ B. An EBS volume can be attached simultaneously to instances across different geographic AWS regions.
✅ C. Users are billed for the total provisioned capacity of an EBS volume, regardless of how much data is actually written.
❌ D. EBS snapshots must be stored in the same Availability Zone as the original source EBS volume.

Q6: Ephemeral Storage (Instance Store) vs. EBS

✅ A. High-speed temporary scratch space for intermediate compiler or video rendering files.

✅ E. Workloads requiring the absolute highest IOPS and lowest latency physically possible on the host.
⚠️ Penalty Trap Alert (-0.5 pts): Do NOT select C or D. Instance Store data is completely erased when an instance stops or the host blade crashes.
